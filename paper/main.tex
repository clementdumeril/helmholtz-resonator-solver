\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The natural frequency of a Helmholtz resonator depends on an end correction that lumped
models assume. I compute it instead with two independent axisymmetric finite-difference
solvers for an open-neck resonator: a frequency-domain solver that closes the mouth with
the radiation impedance of a baffled piston, and a time-domain leapfrog solver that meshes
the exterior half-space and lets the resonator select its own frequency after a broadband
pulse. Manufactured solutions give observed orders of \mmsHarmonicOrder{} and
\mmsTransientOrder{}; the time-domain test also exposed a half-cell offset of the rigid
walls, an $O(h)$ geometric error that is removed here by Richardson extrapolation. The two
solvers then give \fExt{} and \tExt{}~Hz, with grid uncertainties (GCI) of \fGCI\% and
\tGCI\% and a further \fZeroSpread\% from the outer boundary of the time-domain solver; they
differ by \solverGap\%. A study of three outer boundaries shows that the original sponge
layer reflected \reflSponge\% of the incident wave, which explained a factor-six spread in
the computed radiation $Q$. Compared with published measurements, the solver is within
0.9\% and 2.2\% of two resonators of Selamet et al.\ with no fitted parameter, and is
10--13\% above an Erlenmeyer flask. The solvers are lossless, so damping is not predicted.
A training-free test shows that neural bases fit the field to better than 0.4\% while the
PDE residual of that fit is \basesResMin--\basesResMax{} times that of the zero field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A Helmholtz resonator is a cavity of volume $V$ connected to the outside through a neck of
length $L$ and cross-section $S=\pi a^2$. The air plug in the neck oscillates against the
compressibility of the air in the cavity, and the lossless lumped model gives
\begin{equation}
  f_0 = \frac{c}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{S}{V\,(L+\Delta L)}} ,
  \label{eq:lumped}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta L$, the end correction, accounts for the air that moves with the plug just
outside each end of the neck \citep{helmholtz1860,rayleigh1896,ingard1953}. $\Delta L$ is of
the order of the neck radius and is usually taken from tables. For a neck as short as the
one studied here ($L=4a$), it lowers $f_0$ by about 15\%.

A numerical model can compute $\Delta L$ instead of assuming it, but only if its own error
is known. The question addressed here is therefore narrow: can a finite-difference model of
an open resonator predict $f_0$ with a stated numerical uncertainty, and where does it stop
agreeing with measurements? The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item two independent axisymmetric finite-difference solvers, one in the frequency domain
    with an impedance condition at the mouth and one in the time domain with a meshed
    exterior and three interchangeable outer boundaries (sponge, first-order Mur, PML);
  \item a verification sequence (manufactured solutions, Richardson extrapolation with a
    grid convergence index, cross-solver comparison, outer-boundary study) in which the
    manufactured solution revealed a half-cell offset of the rigid walls;
  \item a comparison with three published measured resonators, two matched and one missed,
    and a training-free measurement of how well neural bases represent the computed field
    and its PDE residual (Appendix~\ref{app:bases}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{End corrections and measured resonators.}
Rayleigh derived the end correction of a flanged pipe \citep{rayleigh1896}; Ingard studied
the theory and design of resonators, including the end corrections on both sides of the neck
\citep{ingard1953}; Morse and Ingard give the radiation impedance of a baffled piston used
here \citep{morse1968}. Selamet et al.\ studied concentric cylindrical resonators with
two-dimensional analytical models, a three-dimensional boundary-element method and
experiments \citep{selamet1997}; two of their configurations are used in
Section~\ref{sec:published}. Moloney measured quality factors of glass bottles and found
them slightly lower than his predictions \citep{moloney2004}. Indenbom and Pogossian
measured the resonance of a filled Erlenmeyer flask \citep{indenbom2023}; its empty case is
used here.

\paragraph{Verification.}
The distinction between code verification, solution verification and validation follows
Roache \citep{roache1998} and Oberkampf and Roy \citep{oberkampf2010}. Code verification uses
the method of manufactured solutions \citep{roache2002}. Discretisation uncertainty is
reported with the grid convergence index (GCI) \citep{roache1994,celik2008}.

\paragraph{Open boundaries.}
The first-order absorbing condition of Mur \citep{mur1981} is exact only at normal
incidence. The perfectly matched layer (PML) of B\'erenger \citep{berenger1994} is
reflectionless in the continuous problem for a planar interface; on curved boundaries it
requires a curvilinear stretch \citep{collino1998}. The time-domain PML used here is the
unsplit second-order form for the scalar wave equation with two auxiliary fields
\citep{grote2010}.

\paragraph{Neural PDE solvers.}
Physics-informed neural networks minimise the PDE residual over the parameters of a
network \citep{raissi2019}. Their difficulties on oscillatory problems are often attributed
to spectral bias \citep{rahaman2019,wang2021}, with remedies such as sine activations
\citep{sitzmann2020} and domain decomposition \citep{moseley2023}. Trefftz methods use
functions that satisfy the homogeneous Helmholtz equation exactly \citep{hiptmair2016}.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Geometry.} The resonator is axisymmetric (Table~\ref{tab:params}): a cylindrical
neck of radius $a=10$~mm and length $L=40$~mm opens at $z=0$ into a rigid baffle and joins a
cylindrical cavity of radius $R_c=40$~mm and height 80~mm. All walls are rigid. The air is
lossless, with $c=343$~m/s and $\rho=1.204$~kg/m$^3$.

\paragraph{Equations.} In the time domain the acoustic pressure obeys
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t^2 p = c^2 \nabla^2 p + F(\mathbf{x},t),
  \qquad
  \nabla^2 p = \partial_r^2 p + \frac{1}{r}\partial_r p + \partial_z^2 p ,
  \label{eq:wave}
\end{equation}
and in the frequency domain, at angular frequency $\omega$ and $k=\omega/c$,
\begin{equation}
  \nabla^2 p + k^2 p = -F(\mathbf{x}) .
  \label{eq:helmholtz}
\end{equation}
On the axis, $(1/r)\,\partial_r p$ is indeterminate; since $\partial_r p=0$ there,
L'H\^opital's rule gives $(1/r)\,\partial_r p \to \partial_r^2 p$, so
$\nabla^2 p = 2\,\partial_r^2 p + \partial_z^2 p$ at $r=0$. Rigid walls impose
$\partial_n p = 0$.

\paragraph{Radiation.} The two solvers model the exterior differently, so their agreement
tests the radiation model. The frequency-domain solver stops at the mouth and applies the
low-frequency impedance of a baffled piston \citep{morse1968},
\begin{equation}
  Z_r = \rho c\left[\tfrac12 (ka)^2 + \mathrm{i}\,\tfrac{8}{3\pi} ka\right],
  \label{eq:zr}
\end{equation}
as a Robin condition $\partial_z p = \alpha p$ on $z=0$, $r\le a$, with
$\alpha = \mathrm{i}\omega\rho/Z_r$. Its reactance contains the exterior end correction
$8a/(3\pi)$, so this solver imposes the exterior part of $\Delta L$. The time-domain solver
meshes a baffled exterior half-space and computes the whole end correction; its outer
boundary has to absorb the outgoing wave.

\paragraph{Reference values.} Two lumped references are used, and neither is a measurement.
(i) $\Delta L = 0.85a + 0.66a = 1.51a$, the flanged exterior correction \citep{rayleigh1896}
plus an interior correction of $0.66a$, which gives $f_0=\fTheo$~Hz. The $0.66a$ is close to
the $0.67a$ fitted with this solver's own Dirichlet-mouth runs (Appendix~\ref{app:neck}), so
agreement with this reference is partly circular. (ii) Ingard's interior correction
$0.85a\,(1-1.25\,a/R_c) = \dLIngardInt a$ \citep{ingard1953} with the same exterior term gives
$\Delta L = \dLIngard a$ and $f_0 = \fIngard$~Hz, independent of this work.

\section{Numerical method}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_domains.pdf}
  \caption{Computational domains on the meridian plane. (a) Time-domain solver: open neck,
  meshed exterior with a rigid baffle at $z=0$, absorbing layer (shaded) on the outer
  boundaries, Ricker source (star) and the three probes (dots). (b) Frequency-domain solver:
  the domain stops at the mouth, which carries the radiation impedance; colour shows $|p|$
  at 209.84~Hz on the 0.5~mm grid.}
  \label{fig:domains}
\end{figure}

Both solvers use a uniform grid of step $h$ on the meridian plane (Fig.~\ref{fig:domains})
and the standard second-order stencil of the axisymmetric Laplacian, with the radial
part written as $4(p_1-p_0)/h^2$ on the axis.

\paragraph{Frequency domain.} The operator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:helmholtz} is assembled as a
sparse complex matrix. A rigid wall is imposed by folding the ghost node onto the diagonal:
the coefficient of the missing neighbour is added to the diagonal instead of appearing
off-diagonal, which enforces a zero flux across the face half a cell beyond the last fluid
node. The Robin condition at the mouth eliminates the ghost node through
$p_{-1} = p_1 - 2h\alpha p_0$. Because $k^2$ and $\alpha$ depend on $\omega$, each frequency
requires a new sparse LU factorisation (SciPy): 0.1~s on the 1~mm grid (4\,961 nodes), 4~s on
the 0.25~mm grid (77\,441 nodes). A Gaussian volume source of width 10~mm drives the cavity;
$f_0$ is the peak of the cavity pressure, located by a three-point parabola on a 0.05~Hz
sweep, and the radiation $Q$ is $f_0$ over the $-3$~dB width. A second configuration of the
same scheme, with the mouth set to $p=1$ (Dirichlet), is used for the neck-length study and
for Selamet's side-branch resonators.

\paragraph{Time domain.} Equation~\eqref{eq:wave} is advanced by explicit leapfrog with
$\Delta t = 0.5\,h/(c\sqrt2)$. The Laplacian is written in masked finite volumes: fluxes
through faces between a fluid and a solid cell are zero, which again places each wall on the
face half a cell beyond the last node. The neck opens onto an exterior of radius 120~mm and
depth 100~mm terminated by 30~mm sponge layers. A Ricker pulse centred at 250~Hz is emitted
55~mm in front of the mouth, so the resonator is excited over a broad band and rings at its
own frequency after the pulse has passed. $f_0$ and $Q$ are obtained by fitting
$A e^{-t/\theta}\cos(2\pi f_0 t+\phi)$ to the cavity probe for $t \ge 45$~ms, with
$Q = \pi f_0 \theta$. The fit locates $f_0$ well below the 4.9~Hz resolution of an FFT over
the same window.

\paragraph{Outer boundaries.} Three terminations share one solver core: (i) a sponge, i.e.\
a damping term $\sigma\,\partial_t p$ with $\sigma = \sigma_{\max} d^2$ across the layer, as
in the solver above; (ii) the first-order Mur condition $\partial_t p + c\,\partial_n p = 0$
on the outer ring \citep{mur1981}; (iii) the unsplit PML \citep{grote2010}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \partial_t^2 p + (s_r+s_z)\,\partial_t p + s_r s_z\, p &= c^2\nabla^2 p + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi},\\
  \partial_t \psi_r + s_r \psi_r = c^2 (s_z-s_r)\,\partial_r p, \qquad
  \partial_t \psi_z + s_z \psi_z &= c^2 (s_r-s_z)\,\partial_z p,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with quadratic profiles $s_r$, $s_z$. This is the Cartesian stretch applied in the $(r,z)$
plane: the $1/r$ term of a cylindrical PML \citep{collino1998} is left out.

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

\subsection{Code verification: manufactured solutions}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_mms.pdf}
  \caption{Manufactured solutions. (a) Frequency-domain scheme on a smooth domain with
  Dirichlet edges. (b) Time-domain scheme with rigid walls: matched to the nominal wall
  position the error converges at first order; matched to the wall at $R+h/2$ it converges
  at second order.}
  \label{fig:mms}
\end{figure}

For the frequency-domain scheme, the solution $p_e=\cos(\pi r/R)\cos(\pi z/H)$ on a
$40\times120$~mm rectangle, regular on the axis, is imposed through its source term at the
wavenumber of 235~Hz. Over four grids from 4 to 0.5~mm the RMS error decreases at order
\mmsHarmonicOrder{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:mms}a). This test uses Dirichlet edges and therefore does
not exercise the wall or mouth treatment.

The time-domain test does exercise the walls. It uses
$p = J_0(\lambda r)\cos\!\big(k_z(z+z_0)\big)\,T(t)$ in a closed cylinder, with $\lambda$
chosen so that $\partial_r p=0$ on the lateral wall and $k_z$ so that $\partial_z p = 0$ on
both ends, and $T(t)=u^3e^{-u}$, $u=t/\tau$, with $\tau$ matched to the wave period so that
$\partial_t^2 p$ and $c^2\nabla^2 p$ are of the same size. When the manufactured solution
places the walls at the nominal radius $R$ and height $H$, the observed order is
\mmsNominalOrder{} on four grids (Fig.~\ref{fig:mms}b). When it places them at $R+h/2$ and
$H+h$, the observed order is \mmsTransientOrder{} and the error at $h=2$~mm is
\mmsErrorRatio{} times smaller. The interior scheme is second order; the first-order error
comes from the wall position. The frequency-domain scheme has the same convention through
its ghost-node folding. Both solvers therefore simulate a resonator whose dimensions are
enlarged by $O(h)$, and the full discretisation of the resonator is first order in $h$ by
construction. Aligning the cell faces with the nominal walls would remove this term and
should restore second order; it was not done here, and the offset is handled by
extrapolation instead.

\subsection{Solution verification: grid convergence}
\label{sec:gci}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig_convergence.pdf}
  \caption{Natural frequency of the reference resonator against grid step for both solvers,
  with the Richardson extrapolations at $h=0$ and their GCI, and the semi-empirical lumped
  reference (i).}
  \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

On three grids with refinement ratio 2, the observed order $p$, the extrapolated value and
the fine-grid GCI are \citep{roache1994,celik2008}
\begin{equation}
  p = \frac{\ln\big|(f_3-f_2)/(f_2-f_1)\big|}{\ln 2},\quad
  f_{h\to0} = f_1 + \frac{f_1-f_2}{2^p-1},\quad
  \mathrm{GCI} = 1.25\,\frac{|f_2-f_1|/f_1}{2^p-1},
\end{equation}
with $f_1$ on the finest grid. Since the expected order is 1, successive differences should
halve with each refinement. The ratios of successive differences are \fRatioA{} and
\fRatioB{} for the frequency-domain solver and \tRatioA{} for the time-domain solver, close
to 2, so the three-grid GCI with a safety factor of 1.25 is applicable.

Table~\ref{tab:gci} and Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence} give the results. For the
frequency-domain solver a fourth grid at 0.25~mm gives a second triplet with $p=\fOrderB$;
it moves the extrapolated value by \fExtShift\%, well inside the GCI. A 0.25~mm time-domain
run would take about 8~h on one core and was not done, so the time-domain extrapolation rests
on three grids.

\paragraph{Uncertainty budget.} The time-domain runs of Table~\ref{tab:gci} use the sponge
layer. With the same grid (1~mm) and the three terminations of Section~\ref{sec:pml}, $f_0$
spreads over \fZeroSpread\%, which is added to its uncertainty. The two extrapolated values,
$\tExtPrec \pm \tGCI\%$ (grid) $\pm \fZeroSpread\%$ (outer boundary) and
$\fExtPrec \pm \fGCI\%$ (grid), differ by \solverGapPrec\%, inside both bands. Their raw 1~mm
values agree to 0.3\%, but both carry the same wall offset, so that agreement alone says
little; only the extrapolated values are compared.

\subsection{Outer boundary of the time-domain solver}
\label{sec:pml}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_boundaries.pdf}
  \caption{Outer boundaries. (a) Spurious reflection of a 4~kHz-centred Ricker pulse,
  measured at an off-axis probe against the same run on a domain four times larger.
  (b) Radiation $Q$ of the resonator (1~mm grid) against exterior depth. Configuration B

  \label{fig:boundaries}

With the original sponge layer, the radiation $Q$ ranged from \spongeQmin{} to \spongeQmax{}
as the layer strength, thickness and domain size were changed on the 2~mm grid. (The $f_0$
recorded in that study came from an FFT with a 4~Hz bin and cannot show whether the frequency
moved.) To find out why, each termination was tested on an empty exterior box: a pulse is
fired towards the boundary and the probe signal is compared with the same run on a domain
four times larger, from which nothing can return within the 1.4~ms window. The difference is
the spurious reflection, with no analytic reference needed.

The sponge reflected \reflSponge\% of the incident amplitude (Table~\ref{tab:pml},
Fig.~\ref{fig:boundaries}a), enough to explain the spread in $Q$. The PML reflects
\reflPmlDefault\% at the default strength and \reflPml\% after scanning it. Running each wall
alone isolates its limit: \reflFlat\% ($\reflFlatdB$~dB) on the flat boundary
$z=\mathrm{const}$, where the Cartesian stretch is exact, and \reflCurved\%
($\reflCurveddB$~dB) on the cylindrical boundary, where the $1/r$ term is missing. Neither a
stronger profile nor a thicker layer lowers that floor.

The resonator was then run in two configurations (Fig.~\ref{fig:boundaries}b). In
configuration B only the depth changes, from 10 to 18~cm, at a fixed radius of 12~cm. The
PML $Q$ moves by $\driftPml$\%, against $\driftSponge$\% for the sponge and $\driftAbc$\% for
the Mur condition. This only shows that $Q$ is insensitive to the position of the flat
boundary, where the PML is nearly exact. In configuration C the radius grows with the depth
(12, \extRB, \extRC{} and \extRD~cm). At 18~cm depth, moving the cylindrical boundary from 12
to \extRB~cm raises the PML $Q$ from \pmlQdeepB{} to \pmlQdeepC{} ($\pmlRadiusJump$\%): the
curved boundary, which carries the 4\% floor, still controls the damping. Over configuration
C, with the exterior growing from \extFracA{} to \extFracB, \extFracC{} and \extFracD{}
wavelengths (the wavelength is \wavelength~m at 209~Hz), $Q$ goes from \extQA{} to \extQB,
\extQC{} and \extQD, towards the $Q=\qImpedance$ of the frequency-domain solver on the 1~mm
grid (\qImpHalf{} and \qImpQuarter{} on the 0.5 and 0.25~mm grids). The time-domain radiation
$Q$ is therefore not converged. The frequency moves less: \fZeroSpread\% across the three
terminations in configuration B and \extFspread\% over configuration C.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The resonance and the end correction}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_resonance.pdf}
  \caption{(a) Time-domain solver, 1~mm grid: the pulse reaches the exterior probe, then the
  cavity keeps oscillating at its own frequency. (b) Frequency-domain response of the cavity
  on successive grids; the peak moves down as the walls move in.}
  \label{fig:resonance}
\end{figure}

In the time domain the resonator selects its frequency: after the pulse has passed, the
cavity rings at $f_0$ with no frequency imposed anywhere (Fig.~\ref{fig:resonance}a). In
the frequency domain the response is narrow, with a $-3$~dB width of \sweepBand~Hz on the
1~mm grid (Fig.~\ref{fig:resonance}b).

Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:lumped} gives the end correction implied by each frequency. From the
raw 1~mm time-domain value it is \dLraw$a$, \dLrawGap\% below the $1.51a$ of reference (i);
from the extrapolated value it is \dLext$a$. The \dLrawGap\% deficit at 1~mm is the wall
offset. The extrapolated value is \dLextGap\% above reference (i), which is partly circular,
and \dLextGapIngard\% above Ingard's \dLIngard$a$ (reference (ii)); in frequency, both
extrapolated solvers lie \tGapIngard--\fGapIngard\% below the Ingard value of \fIngard~Hz.
With the impedance condition, the frequency-domain study gives a shift in $\Delta L$ of
$0.823a$ between the Dirichlet and the radiating mouth, against $8a/(3\pi)=0.849a$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:zr}. Since that reactance is imposed, this is a consistency check of the chain,
not an independent computation.

formula knows only the volume and returns \selLumped~Hz for both, $\selLumpedDevA$\% and
$\selLumpedDevB$\% from the measurements. The two-dimensional field is within 2.2\% and
0.9\% of both (Table~\ref{tab:published}). This agreement should not be over-read. The
values come from the raw 1~mm grid, which on the reference geometry sits about 3\% above the
extrapolated frequency, and the Dirichlet mouth leaves out the end correction on the duct
side of the neck. Both effects raise the computed frequency, so a converged computation with
the duct included would land lower, possibly below the measurements; the present agreement
may be partly fortuitous.

Indenbom and Pogossian measured an empty 100~mL Erlenmeyer flask at 351~Hz
\citep{indenbom2023}, where the lumped formula gives 391~Hz; my lumped model gives
\flaskLumped~Hz. The flask is conical and my cavity is a cylinder, so I replaced it by a
cylinder of equal volume and swept its aspect ratio. The solver stays between \flaskMin{}
and \flaskMax~Hz for every shape (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}b), \flaskDevMin--\flaskDevMax\%
above the measurement, so resolving the field near the neck does not close the gap. In terms
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lumped}, the measurement corresponds to an effective neck of about 54~mm
instead of 43~mm. The cause is not established; the conical shoulder, which this solver
cannot represent, is one candidate.

For damping, the same paper gives a half-width that brackets the measured $Q$ between
\qFlaskLo{} and \qFlaskHi{} (the paper does not state whether the width is taken at half
amplitude or half power). An estimate combining viscothermal losses in the neck and radiation
gives about \qFlaskPred{}: the model is optimistic, in the same direction as Moloney's
comparison \citep{moloney2004}. Neither solver contains viscothermal losses.

\subsection{Representing the field with neural bases}

Appendix~\ref{app:bases} projects the frequency-domain field onto \basesHidden{} functions
from each of five neural bases and a Trefftz basis, without training. The projections fit
the field to \basesErrMin--\basesErrMax\%, but their PDE residual is \basesResMin{} to
\basesResMax{} times that of the zero field. The fits that minimise the residual instead
reach \minResLo--\minResHi\% of the zero-field residual but miss the field entirely (field
error from \minResErrLo\% to $\minResErrHi$\%, the latter for tanh). These numbers concern projections of a discrete field onto
fixed random bases; no network is trained.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\paragraph{What the results support.} Two solvers with different discretisations and
different radiation models agree on the natural frequency of the reference resonator to
\solverGap\%, inside their uncertainties of about 2\%. The verification sequence found an
error that the cross-check alone would have hidden: both solvers carry the same half-cell
wall offset, so their raw values agree better than either agrees with its own converged
value.

\paragraph{What they do not support.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Damping.} Both solvers are lossless. The viscothermal boundary layer in the
    neck (about 0.14~mm at this frequency) is the dominant loss and is only estimated
    analytically. The $Q$ values above are radiation-only and are not predictions of a real
    resonator.
  \item \emph{Grid convergence.} The full discretisation is first order because of the wall
    offset and the staircase walls. The time-domain extrapolation rests on three grids and
    carries an extra \fZeroSpread\% from the outer boundary.
  \item \emph{Reference values.} The semi-empirical $1.51a$ uses an interior correction close
    to this solver's own fit; against Ingard's independent value the end correction differs
    by \dLextGapIngard\%.
  \item \emph{Geometry.} Cavities are cylinders. The flask miss is not explained.
  \item \emph{Outer boundary.} The Cartesian PML has a 4\% reflection floor on the curved
    boundary, the reflection was measured with a 4~kHz-centred pulse whose wavelength is much
    shorter than at the resonance, and the radiation $Q$ is not converged in exterior size.
  \item \emph{Comparison data.} All measurements are other authors'. The two matched cases
    are side-branch resonators computed with a Dirichlet mouth on a single grid, so their
    agreement may be partly fortuitous.
\end{itemize}

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion and future work}

A finite-difference model of an open Helmholtz resonator predicts its natural frequency
with a stated uncertainty of about 2\%, provided that the half-cell wall offset revealed by
the manufactured solution is accounted for, here by extrapolation. The end correction
computed by the meshed exterior, \dLext$a$, lies between the semi-empirical value
($+\dLextGap\%$) and Ingard's ($+\dLextGapIngard\%$). Two published resonators are matched
to 0.9--2.2\% under the caveats above, one is missed by about 11\%, and damping is outside
the scope of these lossless models. The next steps are a wall treatment aligned with cell
faces to recover second-order convergence, a cylindrical PML to remove the 4\% floor, a
converged side-branch computation for Selamet's resonators, a viscothermal boundary-layer
model in the neck, and a ring-down measurement of a real resonator, for which the protocol
and analysis script are in the repository.

\section{Representing the field with neural bases}
\label{app:bases}

\begin{figure}[!b]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_bases.pdf}
  \caption{Least-squares fits with \basesHidden{} functions of each family. Bars: projection
  of the field; crosses: the fit that minimises the PDE residual. (a) Relative error on $p$.
  (b) PDE residual in multiples of that of the zero field (dashed line).}
  \label{fig:bases}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Set-up.} The target is the frequency-domain field on the 1~mm grid at
209.84~Hz, which is that grid's own resonance; on the 0.5~mm grid the same frequency is off
resonance and the cavity response is about \offResRatio{} times weaker. The target is
therefore a discrete solution, not the converged field. Six families of \basesHidden{}
functions are taken at random initialisation: a tanh MLP, SIREN with $\omega_0=5$ and 8, Gabor
atoms, a three-zone partition of unity of sine networks in the spirit of FBPINNs, and a
Trefftz basis $J_0(\kappa_m r)Z_m(z)$. For each, two least-squares problems are solved:
(i) the projection of the field onto the span, reporting its relative field error and its
residual $\lVert \nabla^2\tilde p + k^2 \tilde p + F\rVert / \lVert F\rVert$, computed by
automatic differentiation on 900 points of the fluid domain; (ii) the fit that minimises that
residual over the span, reporting the same two numbers. A residual of 1 is what the zero
field achieves. No boundary terms are included, and no network is trained.

\paragraph{Results.} The projections fit the field to \basesErrMin--\basesErrMax\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bases}a), with residuals \basesResMin{} to \basesResMax{} times that of the
zero field (Fig.~\ref{fig:bases}b). The minimum-residual fits reach \minResLo--\minResHi\% of
the zero-field residual, but their field error is between \minResErrLo\% and $\minResErrHi$\% (tanh): without boundary
terms the interior residual does not determine the field, and neither fit comes close to
satisfying both objectives. The conditioning of the residual operator varies by
eleven orders of magnitude between families (from $\kappaMin$ to $\kappaMax$), with sine
activations far better conditioned than tanh, and this does not change the picture. The
Trefftz basis satisfies $\nabla^2\phi + k^2\phi = 0$ exactly, so both of its residuals equal
that of the zero field; it cannot carry the source and fits the field only to
\trefftzErr\%.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} On the sample points $\lVert k^2 p\rVert/\lVert F\rVert \approx
\kTwoPoverF$, so a smooth error of 0.15\% on $p$ would contribute only about 7\% of $\lVert F
\rVert$ through the $k^2p$ term. The residual is dominated instead by the Laplacian of the
error: an error of relative size $\varepsilon$ varying on a length $\ell$ gives a residual of
order $\varepsilon\,(\lVert k^2p\rVert/\lVert F\rVert)/(k\ell)^2$. For the best basis (Gabor)
this matches the measured residual for $\ell \approx \errScale$~mm, of the order of the grid
step. Much of the residual therefore plausibly comes from grid-scale structure of the
discrete target (for example at the staircase walls), which no smooth basis reproduces. The test shows that projecting this
discrete field onto these bases gives an unusable residual, and that minimising the interior
residual alone does not recover the field; it does not show that the bases cannot represent
the Laplacian of the continuous solution.


\section{Neck-length study with the Dirichlet mouth}
\label{app:neck}

With the Dirichlet mouth, the three-grid study over five neck lengths from 2 to 6~cm gives
observed orders between 0.87 and 0.95 and GCIs between 2.0\% and 2.3\% (mean 2.1\%;
notebook section 4). A 0.25~mm grid for $L=2$ and 4~cm moves the observed order from 0.91 to
1.00 and from 0.87 to 1.01, and the extrapolations by 0.20\% and 0.36\%. Fitted on the
extrapolated frequencies, a free power law $AL^b$ and the effective-length model
$A/\sqrt{L+\Delta L}$ cannot be told apart ($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=0.3$); the effective-length
fit gives $\Delta L = 0.67a$ and $A$ close to the theoretical \aTheory~m$^{1/2}$/s.
